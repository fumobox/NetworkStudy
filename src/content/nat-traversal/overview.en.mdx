## Why peer-to-peer needs help

In the NAT theme, a PC behind a NAT could reach any server, but nothing on the Internet could start a connection to the PC (without port forwarding). A video call between two PCs behind two NATs has exactly that problem on both sides: each PC has only a private address, and each NAT drops packets it was not expecting. The app’s signalling server can pass messages between the two PCs, but carrying all the audio and video through it would be slow and expensive.

**NAT traversal** is the set of tricks that lets the two PCs find a path anyway: **STUN** to learn the public addresses, **TURN** as a relay when nothing else works, and **ICE** to try all the possibilities in order.

## STUN: “what address do you see?”

A PC sends a STUN **Binding Request** to a STUN server. The server answers with the source address and port it saw, in **XOR-MAPPED-ADDRESS**: that is the PC’s address on the outside of its NAT, a **server-reflexive** candidate. The address is XORed with the **magic cookie** 0x2112A442 only because some NATs rewrite anything that looks like their own address, even inside packets; XOR is not encryption.

STUN alone does not get anyone through a NAT. It only tells each PC what the outside sees.

## How NATs behave: mapping and filtering

RFC 4787 separates two behaviours:

- **Mapping**: which external port the NAT uses. With **endpoint-independent mapping**, the same internal port always gets the same external port, whatever the destination, so the address STUN reports also works for the other PC. With **address- and port-dependent mapping** (often called a “symmetric” NAT), every new destination gets a new external port, so the address STUN reports is useless for anyone else.
- **Filtering**: which incoming packets the NAT lets in. With **address- and port-dependent filtering**, only packets from an address and port the PC has already sent to get in.

That is why **hole punching** needs both sides to send: PC A’s first packet is dropped by NAT B, but it opens NAT A for PC B, and PC B’s packet then opens NAT B for PC A.

## TURN: a relay you have to ask for

When no direct path works, a **TURN** server relays the traffic. The PC sends **Allocate** and, after a **401** challenge, authenticates with long-term credentials. The server reserves a **relayed address** for it (here 198.51.100.3:55000) for 600 seconds.

The server only relays packets from addresses the PC has given a **permission** (CreatePermission). A permission is for an IP address, whatever the port, and lasts 300 seconds. Data can be wrapped in **Send** and **Data** indications, or, after **ChannelBind**, in **ChannelData** with just a 4-byte header. Allocations, permissions and channels each expire on their own timer and must be refreshed (ChannelBind also refreshes the permission for that peer); sending data refreshes none of them.

Relaying costs the server bandwidth and adds delay, so it is the last resort.

## ICE: try every pair

**ICE** gathers every candidate each PC has: **host** (its own address), **server-reflexive** (from STUN) and **relayed** (from TURN). It gives each a priority, pairs them with the other side’s candidates, and runs **connectivity checks** (STUN Binding requests between the PCs) in priority order. A check that reveals an address nobody knew about creates a **peer-reflexive** candidate. When a pair works in both directions, the **controlling** side (the one that made the offer) **nominates** it with USE-CANDIDATE, and media flows on that pair.

## Signalling and SDP

ICE does not say how the candidates reach the other PC. The app sends them in an SDP **offer** and **answer** through its own signalling server, for example over WebSocket. **Trickle ICE** sends each candidate as soon as it is found instead of waiting for all of them.

## Keeping the path alive

While the call lasts, each side sends checks every 4–6 seconds to confirm that the other still wants the traffic (**consent freshness**); consent expires after 30 seconds without a response. On an idle pair, a keepalive every 15 seconds keeps the NAT mappings open. On the relay, the PC must refresh its allocation, its permissions and its channels before each one runs out.

## What to look for in the walkthrough

- The **NAT tables**: the same external port for every destination with endpoint-independent mapping, a new one for each destination with the “symmetric” NAT.
- The **XOR-MAPPED-ADDRESS** bytes and the decoded address, and the first check **dropped** at NAT B.
- The **checklist** states, the **prflx** candidates that appear with the “symmetric” NAT, and the **selected pair**.
- On the server: the **permissions** (an IP without a port), the **channel**, and the lifetimes after four minutes.
- In “What if…”, the “symmetric” NAT (the relay wins), **UDP blocked** (TURN over TLS), and a **forgotten permission** (PC B’s packets are dropped).

## Try it yourself

There is no built-in command for STUN, but a browser shows ICE at work during a video call:

- In Chrome, open `chrome://webrtc-internals` in another tab during a call. The ICE candidates are listed with their types: `host` (your own address), `srflx` (the address a STUN server saw) and `relay` (an address on a TURN server). The selected candidate pair shows which one won.
- In Firefox, the page is `about:webrtc`.
- In Wireshark, the display filter `stun` shows the STUN binding requests and responses, and the TURN messages too.

## About this page

- IPv4 only, one media stream, and UDP only for ICE. Both PCs run full ICE, which is why the offerer is the controlling side.
- The signalling server is not drawn, and the candidates are sent after all of them are gathered (no Trickle ICE).
- Only PC A uses TURN. NAT A filters by address and port in every case, so that the “symmetric” NAT on the other side really forces the relay.
- Transaction IDs, ufrags, passwords and ports are example values; real ones are random.
