## Why a handshake?

TCP delivers a stream of bytes reliably and in order. To do that, each side numbers the bytes it sends with **sequence numbers**, and the other side reports how far it has received with **acknowledgment numbers**.

Before any data can flow, both sides need to tell each other where their numbering starts. That starting point is the **initial sequence number (ISS)**, and exchanging it is the job of the three-way handshake.

## The three segments

1. **SYN** — the client sends its ISS.
2. **SYN, ACK** — the server sends its own ISS and acknowledges the client’s (client ISS + 1, because the SYN itself uses one sequence number).
3. **ACK** — the client acknowledges the server’s ISS.

After step 3, both sides know each other’s starting point and the connection is **ESTABLISHED**.

## What to look for in the walkthrough

- How **Seq** and **Ack** change from segment to segment in the packet details.
- How each side’s **state** changes (CLOSED → SYN-SENT → ESTABLISHED on the client, LISTEN → SYN-RECEIVED → ESTABLISHED on the server).
- In the “What if…” options: what the **retransmission timer** does when a segment is lost, and what a closed port answers.

## Try it yourself

`nc -vz` opens a TCP connection and closes it again at once. Try an open port, then a closed one on your own computer:

```sh
nc -vz example.com 443
nc -vz 127.0.0.1 9
```

Output (macOS 26, 2026-09-29):

```
Connection to example.com port 443 [tcp/https] succeeded!
nc: connectx to 127.0.0.1 port 9 (tcp) failed: Connection refused
```

- “succeeded” means the three-way handshake completed. “Connection refused” means the other side answered the SYN with a reset: nothing listens on that port, as in the closed-port option of the walkthrough.
- If a firewall silently drops the SYN instead, nc waits and times out: the SYN is retransmitted, as in the lost-SYN options.
- Linux’s nc prints the same result in slightly different words. To see the segments themselves, capture with Wireshark and use the display filter `tcp.flags.syn == 1 || tcp.flags.reset == 1`. Wireshark shows sequence numbers relative to the first one (starting at 0) by default.
