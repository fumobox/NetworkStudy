## Anyone can write any From

SMTP, the protocol that moves mail between servers, has no way to verify who the sender is. A server can write any address in MAIL FROM (the envelope sender, where bounces go) and any address in the From: line the reader sees. Three mechanisms, all published in DNS by the domain owner, let a receiving server tell genuine mail from forged mail.

## SPF: which servers may send

**SPF** lets a domain list the servers that may send its mail, in a TXT record such as `v=spf1 ip4:192.0.2.25 -all`. The receiver takes the domain of **MAIL FROM**, fetches its record, and checks the connecting IP address against it, left to right. The result is **pass**, **fail** (`-all`), **softfail** (`~all`), **neutral** (`?all`), **none** (no record), or temperror / permerror.

SPF never looks at the From: line. And because it is about the connecting server, it breaks when mail is **forwarded**: the forwarder is not in the original sender’s list.

## DKIM: a signature on the message

**DKIM** signs the message itself. The sending server adds a **DKIM-Signature** header: the signing domain (`d=`), the **selector** (`s=`, which key), the list of signed header fields (`h=`, which must include From), a hash of the body (`bh=`), and the signature (`b=`). The receiver fetches the public key from `<selector>._domainkey.<domain>`, recomputes the hashes, and checks the signature.

DKIM survives forwarding, because the message does not change. It breaks when the message is **modified**, for example by a mailing list that adds a tag to the Subject and a footer to the body.

## DMARC: tying it to From

Neither SPF nor DKIM checks the domain the reader actually sees. **DMARC** does: it takes the domain of From:, fetches the policy at `_dmarc.<domain>`, and passes only if SPF or DKIM passed for a domain **aligned** with it. With relaxed alignment (the default) the same organizational domain is enough, so `mail.example.com` aligns with `example.com`; strict alignment needs an exact match. A pass for an unrelated domain counts for nothing.

When DMARC fails, the policy `p=` says what the domain owner would like: **none** (just monitor; the aggregate reports that receivers send to `rua=` show what fails), **quarantine** (treat as suspicious, such as the spam folder), or **reject** (refuse it; since From: is only known after DATA, the refusal is the reply to the end of the message, such as `550 5.7.1`). The receiver decides how to apply it: RFC 9989 says receivers must not reject on p=reject alone, and without other evidence must treat such mail as quarantine, although many still reject.

DMARC is now specified in RFC 9989 (with RFC 9990 and RFC 9991 for reports), which replaced RFC 7489.

## What DMARC does not do

A DMARC pass means the From domain is genuine; it does not mean the mail is harmless. A look-alike domain registered by an attacker can pass SPF, DKIM and DMARC for itself. And strict policies hurt legitimate mail that travels indirectly: mailing lists usually work around this by rewriting From: to their own address. **ARC** (RFC 8617), which records the results the list saw, is an experimental alternative that only helps if the receiver trusts the list.

## What to look for in the walkthrough

- The **MAIL FROM** in the envelope and the **From:** in the message: two different identities.
- The three DNS lookups, and the receiver’s **checks** table filling in: SPF, DKIM, then the alignment for DMARC.
- In “What if…”, a **forged** message, a **forwarded** one (SPF fails, DKIM saves it), a **mailing list** (SPF passes for the wrong domain, DKIM breaks), and the three **policies**.

## Try it yourself

SPF and DMARC policies are TXT records anyone can look up:

```sh
dig +short TXT example.com
dig +short TXT _dmarc.example.com
```

Trimmed output (macOS 26, 2026-09-30; example.com also has an unrelated TXT record):

```
$ dig +short TXT example.com
"v=spf1 -all"
$ dig +short TXT _dmarc.example.com
"v=DMARC1;p=reject;sp=reject;adkim=s;aspf=s"
```

- `v=spf1 -all` says no server at all may send mail for example.com: a domain that sends no mail can protect itself this way. `p=reject` asks receivers to reject messages that fail DMARC, `sp=reject` does the same for subdomains, and `adkim=s` and `aspf=s` require strict alignment.
- A DKIM key is found from a received message: its `DKIM-Signature` header names the selector (`s=`) and the domain (`d=`), and the key is at `selector._domainkey.domain`. In a mail client, “show original” (or “view source”) shows the receiver’s `Authentication-Results` header with the results `spf=`, `dkim=` and `dmarc=`.
