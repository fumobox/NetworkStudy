## What a cumulative ACK can’t say

A TCP ACK is **cumulative**: its Ack number is the first byte the receiver is still missing. When one segment is lost, every segment that arrives after it produces the same ACK again: a **duplicate ACK**. The Ack can’t move past the hole, and it can’t say what arrived after it.

## Fast retransmit

Waiting for the retransmission timer (RTO, at least 1 second) would be slow. Instead, when the sender sees the **third duplicate ACK**, it concludes that the segment was lost and resends it at once. It waits for three because one or two duplicates may just mean the segments were **reordered** on the way.

## One hole per round trip

If two segments from the same window are lost, duplicate ACKs only reveal the first one. After the retransmission fills it, the Ack jumps forward but stops at the second hole: a **partial ACK**. Only then does the sender (NewReno) resend the second segment, one round trip later.

## SACK

With **SACK** (Selective Acknowledgment), both sides agree in the SYN segments (SACK-Permitted), and each ACK can carry up to four **blocks** (three when the Timestamps option is also used) of data received after the hole. A block is written as the first byte received and the first byte **not** received after it, for example `3001-5001`. The first block is the one that holds the segment that just arrived.

The sender keeps a **scoreboard** of which segments are SACKed. When enough data after a segment has been SACKed (more than (3 − 1) × MSS bytes), it treats that segment as lost and resends it, without waiting for a partial ACK. Several holes can be repaired in the same round trip.

## When nothing comes after

If the **last** segment is lost, no later segment arrives, so there are no duplicate ACKs and no SACK blocks. The sender has to wait for the RTO. Modern stacks shorten this wait with a tail loss probe (RACK-TLP, RFC 8985).

How cwnd and ssthresh change during recovery is shown in the congestion control theme.

## What to look for in the walkthrough

- The **duplicate ACKs** and their **SACK blocks** growing as segments arrive after the holes.
- The **scoreboard** on the sender: in flight, ACKed, SACKed, retransmitted.
- In “What if…”, **cumulative ACKs only** (the partial ACK costs a round trip), **one** lost segment, and a lost **last** segment (only the RTO helps).

## Try it yourself

```sh
sysctl net.inet.tcp.sack
netstat -s -p tcp
```

Output of the first command (macOS 26, 2026-09-29):

```
$ sysctl net.inet.tcp.sack
net.inet.tcp.sack: 1
```

- 1 means SACK is on: the SYN offers SACK-Permitted, and if the other side agrees, ACKs can carry SACK blocks.
- In the output of `netstat -s -p tcp`, the lines “data packet … retransmitted”, “duplicate ack”, “SACK recovery episode” and “segment rexmit in SACK recovery episodes” count, since the computer started, the events this page draws.
- On Linux, `sysctl net.ipv4.tcp_sack` shows the same setting and `nstat -az | grep -i sack` the counters. In Wireshark, the display filter `tcp.options.sack_le` shows ACKs that carry SACK blocks, and `tcp.analysis.retransmission || tcp.analysis.fast_retransmission` the retransmissions.
